# Interregional trade and migration

Founding campaigns include neighboring administrations with their own harvest, reserve, treasury, and population. A seeded local harvest roll and regional monsoon pressure determine whether each neighbor has food to sell or needs to buy. Settled purchases and sales update both sides. Migration follows modeled food security and access to connected towns. It uses the same abstract population units as the regional simulation.

- **Food supply compact.** Pay for priority access to available partner stocks. Use the shortage-buying food policy to purchase food if your harvest and reserves fall short.
- **Market corridor agreement.** Pay to open a corridor after connecting three towns or completing a road link. Both administrations collect freight revenue while the route stays open. The partner can also buy a unit for reserves in a surplus year.
- **Light politics.** The cabinet can plan around the weather and partner stocks. Signed corridors remain open in poor harvest years.
- **Standard politics.** Partners keep a larger reserve before offering stocks and may restrict routes if their own harvest falls short. Migration reacts more strongly to shortages and connected towns.

## Neighbor investment

Partner treasuries can fund one work at a time. A work debits the partner's own cash when committed and opens two planning years later. The annual brief records commitments and openings. The diplomacy screen carries the delivery date and effect.

| Work | Cost | Effect once open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reserve depots | ₹7 lakh | Raises the partner's food reserve capacity from 12 to 18. A signed food compact can make one additional unit available when the player faces a shortage and the partner has stocks to spare. |
| Freight exchange | ₹10 lakh | Raises the partner's handling capacity by one unit when it can reach the player market. An open signed corridor earns both governments an extra ₹1 lakh per year. |

Light favors freight when a route is viable and storage otherwise. Standard weighs recent shortages, reserve pressure, the type of agreement, and available market demand. It sets aside more cash when the partner needs to buy food. Each administration can build one of each work. Earlier saves start with their existing cash, stocks, and agreements, and the works portfolio grows from that state.

## Joint projects

An administration that has signed a trade agreement and opened its relevant works can propose a joint project. Each side pays its share once. The offer shows the completion year and any limits on funding. The player can fund a joint project while the domestic works office is occupied.

| Agreement | Joint project | Player and partner shares in lakh | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Food supply compact | Shared emergency reserve | ₹12 and ₹6, or ₹8 and ₹6 for autonomous Mayurbhanj | Opens in two years. The partner can hold six more food units. Up to two available units reach the player after local reserves are exhausted and before emergency purchases. |
| Market corridor agreement | Shared freight connection | ₹18 and ₹10, or ₹12 and ₹10 for autonomous Mayurbhanj | Opens in three years. Each government earns ₹2 lakh more per year and the corridor can handle one more food unit when open. |

The annual brief records joint commitments, openings and realized payoffs. A merger retires a joint project between the two administrations when their border disappears. Other agreements and investments continue.

The central campaign can sign a Nagpur freight agreement as Vidarbha passes to Bombay. The new administration receives a census-based share of the works treasury and a modeled share of food reserves. Operating workshops in Vidarbha earn receipts for Bombay. Maharashtra inherits the sites and active agreements in 1960. [Central campaign chronology](central-chronology.md)

## Freight competition

From 1957 under Standard politics, an open nearby freight exchange creates competition for a limited pool of freight orders. The player's administration needs three connected market towns and a connected workshop to compete. Every connected town beyond the first two and every connected workshop adds one unit of capacity. A connected terminal adds three, up to a total capacity of fourteen. Each completed order earns ₹1 lakh. Signed corridor and joint project fees continue to pay under their own terms.

A neighbor's accessible exchange handles three orders. Neighbors can invest ₹14 lakh in a loading yard after handling at least two orders or in time for an upcoming contract, provided their treasury and food situation allow it. The yard adds three units when it opens. The simulation allocates orders in proportion to capacity, with annual rotation when claims tie. Severe floods reduce demand. The Neighbors screen shows the split of orders, capacity and receipts, and the annual brief shows the proceeds in the treasury ledger.

An inland administration can enter the same contest when its own exchange is open and a domestic route connects it to an accessible neighbor exchange. An inland exchange bids for up to two loads. Its loading yard raises that limit to four, subject to the route and neighbor capacities. A shortfall can keep an inland carrier out. Carriers compete for the existing order pool and collect ₹1 lakh for each completed load. Those receipts stay in their treasuries and help finance future loading yards. The market ledger shows receipts and yard delivery status. The annual brief reports how many orders inland rivals carried.

### Regional freight contracts

Standard politics announces the first contract in 1957, for the five years from 1961 through 1965. Further full terms begin in 1966, 1971, 1976 and 1981 if the campaign runs long enough. The forecast compares the player's current works, works due by the award, and the best known neighboring bid. Works must be operating when the award year closes. The domestic award goes to the highest qualified bid, with a minimum of five and rotating tie priority.

The player needs three linked towns and a linked workshop. Each linked workshop adds one bid point, each linked terminal adds three, and towns beyond the first two add up to two points. A neighboring exchange bids three points, with another three from a loading yard and additional capacity from inherited works. The bid scale caps at fourteen. A rival can invest using its own budget and reserves even if the player's food supply is short. The region remains eligible when its operating works meet those conditions after a transfer. Payments pause whenever the winner falls below the minimum or loses access.

The ₹6 lakh yearly fee is a modeled payment for keeping capacity available. The award runs alongside shipments, existing corridor payments and workshop receipts. Fees post to the winner's cash ledger when each year closes. Archived reports keep their original results. An older save already past an award year joins at the next eligible round.

## Domestic food market

### Market funding and northern access

AI administrations now use period fiscal references for their annual market allocations, replacing the flat ₹1 lakh allowance where a reference is recorded. The model takes one tenth of the existing regional works envelope, or a declared share of direct revenue and Union territory Annual Plan observations. A missing earlier reference keeps a labeled modeled allowance. Cash carries forward and each year's allowance posts once. Expand the market budget in Neighbors or the treasury table to inspect the source, year and modeled share. [Fiscal definitions, observations and gaps](market-fiscal-research.md)

Himachal trades through Mandi from 1948, with capacity increasing from one to two units in 1958. The scoped Jammu exchange joins through the Pathankot road in 1949 and gains a three-unit rail route in 1973 after Jammu Tawi's 1972 inauguration. Sikkim joins the domestic graph through Gangtok in 1975. Transport limits bound direct food relief, purchases, sales and freight bids. In a Haryana continuation, the northern exchanges remain distant through Punjab rather than gaining an invented direct route to Jammu. Inherited hill works continue to serve Himachal. ### Rival investment priorities

From 1956 under Standard politics, nearby and domestic market administrations can fund water and crop protection, a technical school and food processing workshops alongside their existing depots, exchanges and loading yards. Every administration uses its own carried treasury and keeps money available for food purchases. It can deliver one investment at a time. A project must finish before the campaign ends.

Recent shortages make protection a priority where flood or dry-season losses are material. A ₹9 lakh protection project takes two years and prevents up to two units of actual weather loss from its opening year. That can free food for trade or reduce purchases. With secure supplies and an operating exchange, a state can spend ₹12 lakh on a technical school. It opens after two years and contributes ₹1 lakh of modeled annual receipts after its first teaching year. A trained workforce and surplus permit a ₹16 lakh processing investment with a three-year delivery schedule. Operating processing adds ₹2 lakh of receipts while the training school remains available.

A trained workforce adds one capacity point at a local exchange and operating processing adds another. Domestic inland loading is bounded by routes and the connecting exchange. Contract forecasts include scheduled school graduation and workshop openings. Schools, processing and protection each show their department colour, funding reason, cost and opening date in Neighbors and the Domestic market ledger. The annual brief highlights nearby openings and keeps the wider national investment record available below.

The simulated division of a distant administration allocates its storage and protection to the agricultural successor, and keeps its training, processing and freight together in the other successor. Each funded work transfers once with its existing delivery dates. The budgets, investment types, allocations and numerical effects are game models.

The western routes change when Bombay divides in 1960, and Punjab and Haryana become separate markets in 1966. ## Union food reserve

In December 1957, the Minister of Food and Agriculture reported that the Union had assembled about half of its intended two-million-ton reserve. The debate named Andhra’s delta, Orissa, Punjab and the Chhattisgarh division of Madhya Pradesh as procurement areas and discussed how drought limited supply. The Estimates Committee described direct procurement and bulk releases to state governments. [Lok Sabha debate, 3 December 1957](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/900163/1/02_III_03-12-1957_p55_p118_PII.pdf), [Estimates Committee report, paragraphs 24–26 and 40–43](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/58034/1/ec_02_126_1960.pdf)

All founding administrations receive notice in 1956. They can sign during 1957 and deliver through 1960. Market access and operating food stores set annual capacity. Each granary supports up to four units and each cooperative depot up to six. The state can route at most one unit for each linked town after the first, capped at six. Works due by year end count, and territory changes update eligible sites.

The Supply agreement can sell up to six units each year and protects four units in state reserves. The Protected reserve option can sell up to three and protects nine. Home consumption comes first. Shipments and ordinary sales can only use stock above the agreed floor. Shipment pays ₹2 lakh per unit delivered. The model uses food units and game prices. It tracks a four-unit opening Union stock earmarked for this campaign’s corridor, with subsequent additions from actual shipments. From 1958 to 1960, unmet needs after local and shared reserves can receive up to three units from the corridor, limited by connected storage, shipping capacity and stock. Release applies to either agreement or an unsigned state with suitable facilities.
